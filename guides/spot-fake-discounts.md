A discount is only real if the price you pay is lower than what you would realistically have paid on another day. That sounds obvious, yet most "70% off" banners are measured against a price almost nobody ever paid. This guide shows the tricks that make ordinary prices look like bargains, and the quick checks that expose them.

## The anchor price problem

Every discount needs a reference price, usually called the "was", "regular" or "list" price. Sellers choose that number. Three common ways it gets inflated:

- **The phantom list price.** The product has a published list price but is always sold below it. A "50% off" claim measured from list is really the normal price.
- **The pre-sale bump.** The price rises a few weeks before a big sale and then "drops" back to where it was. Consumer groups in several countries have documented this pattern around Black Friday.
- **The bundle valuation.** A lifetime deal or bundle lists a "total value" by adding up monthly prices for years, or by valuing extras nobody would buy separately.

The fix is the same for all three: ignore the percentage and judge the final price against the product's real price history and its closest alternatives.

## Five checks that take two minutes

1. **Find the vendor's own pricing page.** If the "deal" is on a third-party store, open the developer's site in another tab. Many vendors sell the same licence directly at the same or a lower price.
2. **Check the billing unit.** "Only $4.99" may be per month, billed annually. Convert everything to the yearly cost you will actually pay.
3. **Read the renewal terms.** Introductory pricing that doubles at renewal is common for VPNs, hosting and creative software. A first-year discount is worth less if you plan to stay for three years.
4. **Look for the same offer at another time of year.** If the product ran the same discount last month and the month before, there is no urgency. Our [sale calendar](../calendar.html) shows when real seasonal lows usually happen.
5. **Compare against a free option.** For many tasks there is a capable free or open-source tool. Check our [free alternatives](../alternatives.html) before paying for something you will use twice a year.

## Countdown timers and "only 3 left"

Digital licences do not run out. A countdown that resets when you reload the page, or a stock counter on a software key, is pressure, not information. Real limited-time offers usually have a published end date that stays the same for everyone. If you are unsure, note the end time, close the tab and come back later. A genuine deal will still show the same deadline.

## Grey-market keys and "too cheap" licences

Some marketplaces sell software keys far below every legitimate price. These keys can come from regional pricing differences, volume-licence programmes, or keys bought with stolen payment cards. The risks are practical, not just ethical:

- The vendor can revoke the key, leaving you with nothing.
- Keys restricted to one region may not activate where you live.
- Support and refunds may be impossible because you are not the vendor's customer.

If a price is far below the vendor's own sale price and the seller is not an authorised reseller, assume the risk is yours.

## Lifetime deals deserve extra scrutiny

A lifetime deal trades a one-time payment for ongoing access. That only works if the company survives and keeps supporting the plan you bought. Before buying, read our [lifetime deal buyer's checklist](lifetime-deals-checklist.html) and run the numbers in the [lifetime deal calculator](../tools/lifetime-deal-calculator.html), which discounts the value for the chance that the product shuts down.

## Red flags at a glance

| Signal | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Discount measured from a list price nobody pays | The saving is invented |
| Price per month shown, billed yearly | The real commitment is 12 times larger |
| Timer resets on reload | Fake urgency |
| No refund window | You carry all the risk |
| Seller is not the developer or an authorised reseller | Licence may be revoked |
| Renewal price hidden or "see terms" | Year two may cost far more |

## A simple rule

Decide what the product is worth to you before you look at the discount. Write down the most you would pay for a year of use. If the deal beats that number, and the checks above pass, buy it.
