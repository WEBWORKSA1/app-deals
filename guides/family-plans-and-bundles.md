Family plans and bundles are the two biggest levers for cutting subscription costs in a household. They work in opposite directions: a family plan shares one service among more people, while a bundle combines several services for one person or household. Both can save a lot, and both can be a waste if you do not check what you actually use.

## Family plans: cost per person

A family plan is worth it when the plan price divided by the people who will actually use it is lower than what they would each pay alone.

| Example | Individual price | Family price | People using it | Cost per person |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Music service | $11 | $17 | 4 | $4.25 |
| Office suite | $10 | $13 | 3 | $4.33 |
| Cloud storage | $3 | $10 | 2 | $5.00 |

The cloud storage example shows the trap: with only two people, the family plan costs more per person than two individual plans would. The [family plan splitter](../tools/family-plan-splitter.html) runs this for any service.

## Share purchases you already made

Apple Family Sharing and Google Play Family Library let a family group share eligible app purchases, so you do not buy the same paid app twice. Both are free to switch on, and many households never do. Family organisers should also turn on purchase approvals for children.

## Bundles: count only what you use

A bundle combines several services, for example Apple One, or a phone plan that includes a streaming service. To evaluate one:

1. List each service in the bundle.
2. Mark the ones you would pay for on their own today.
3. Add up the individual prices of only the marked services.
4. Compare that total with the bundle price.

If the bundle costs less than the services you would buy anyway, it saves money. If you only use one service in the bundle, it almost never does.

## App subscription bundles

Services such as Setapp on Mac and Google Play Pass on Android bundle many third-party apps into one subscription. These are excellent value if you use several included apps regularly, and poor value if you install one and forget the rest. Use the trial to install only the apps you need, then check after a few weeks how many you opened.

## Rules for sharing

Family plans are usually meant for members of one household, and some services check location. Read the terms before adding people outside your home. Each person keeps their own account and data on most family plans, which is better for privacy than sharing one login.

## A household subscription routine

- Keep one shared list of all household subscriptions, who uses each, and who pays.
- Review it every quarter with the [subscription audit](../tools/subscription-audit.html).
- Look for duplicates: two people paying for the same service on different platforms is common.
- When a family member stops using a service, remove them, and check whether a cheaper tier now fits.

Done once, this usually pays for itself the same afternoon.
