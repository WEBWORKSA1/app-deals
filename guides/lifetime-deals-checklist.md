A lifetime deal (often shortened to LTD) gives you ongoing access to a software product for a single payment. For buyers, the appeal is obvious: no monthly bill. For the company selling it, a lifetime deal brings in cash and early users, but every lifetime customer keeps costing money for servers and support without paying again. That tension explains why some lifetime deals become the best purchase you ever made, and others quietly die.

## What "lifetime" usually means

In most terms of service, lifetime means the lifetime of the product, not your lifetime. If the company shuts down, is acquired, or retires the plan you bought, your access can end. Read the deal terms for three things:

- **Whose lifetime?** Look for wording about the product being discontinued.
- **Which plan?** Lifetime buyers are often placed on a specific plan. Check whether future features will be included or reserved for subscribers.
- **What limits?** Seats, projects, storage, monthly credits or AI usage caps are typical. A plan that fits you today may not fit in a year.

## The checklist

1. **Do you need it now?** The most common lifetime-deal regret is buying a tool "for later". If you would not pay a monthly subscription for it this month, skip it.
2. **Is the company real and staffed?** Look for a team page, a public changelog with recent entries, an active help centre and responses to user questions.
3. **How old is the product?** A product with several years of releases is less risky than a launch-week deal.
4. **Does it depend on expensive third parties?** Tools built on paid AI models, SMS or email sending have real per-user costs. Lifetime plans for these are the most likely to be capped or changed later.
5. **Can you export your data?** If the product disappears, you need your work in a standard format.
6. **What is the refund window?** Marketplaces commonly offer a refund period. Put the deadline in your calendar and test the product properly before it passes.
7. **What does the subscription cost?** Your saving is measured against what you would otherwise pay, not against an inflated "total value".

## The break-even maths

Divide the lifetime price by the monthly price of an equivalent subscription. The answer is the number of months you need to use the product before the deal pays for itself.

| Lifetime price | Equivalent monthly plan | Break-even |
|---|---|---|
| $59 | $12 per month | about 5 months |
| $149 | $15 per month | about 10 months |
| $299 | $19 per month | about 16 months |

Then adjust for risk. If you think there is a 20% chance each year that the product shuts down or changes your plan, the expected value of later years falls quickly. ## Stacking and upgrades

Many lifetime deals come in tiers, with higher tiers unlocking more seats or usage. Buy the tier that fits your real usage over the next year, not the highest tier "just in case". If the product earns its place, you can usually upgrade later during the same campaign. After the campaign ends, upgrades may not be possible at the same price.

## When lifetime deals make sense

- Utilities with low running costs, such as desktop apps that run on your own machine.
- Tools you already pay for monthly and plan to keep for years.
- Products with a long track record and a sustainable business behind them.

## When to pass

- Anything you are not going to use this week.
- New products with no changelog or support history.
- Tools whose main feature relies on costly third-party services.
- Deals where the only argument is the size of the percentage.
